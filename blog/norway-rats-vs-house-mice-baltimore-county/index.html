Most of the calls we take in Pikesville and the rest of Baltimore County are one of two animals: the house mouse or the Norway rat. They look different, behave differently, and the right response is different for each, so telling them apart early saves time.

Size and droppings.

Both animals are most active at night, but a Norway rat sighted during the day in a well-lit area is more unusual and often signals an unusually large or crowded colony nearby, since daytime activity means normal nocturnal spacing has broken down.

A house mouse is small, typically under four inches not counting the tail, and its droppings are about the size of a grain of rice, dark and pointed at both ends. A Norway rat is considerably larger, often seven to ten inches not counting the tail, with droppings closer to the size of a raisin, blunt-ended and noticeably bigger than anything a mouse leaves behind.

Mouse droppings are also lighter in color when fresh and darken over a day or two as they dry out, which gives a rough sense of how recently an area has been active rather than left over from weeks ago. A cluster of fresh, dark droppings in the same spot day after day points to a nest nearby, while scattered, uniformly dried droppings often mean the animal has already moved on.

If droppings are scattered in small numbers along a kitchen baseboard, that pattern leans mouse. If they are larger, fewer and concentrated near a basement wall or sill plate, that leans Norway rat.

Where each one travels.

This is the biggest practical difference for a Baltimore County home. Norway rats are ground-level burrowers. They favor foundation cracks, basements, crawl spaces and yard burrows near woodpiles or dense landscaping. They are not strong climbers compared to roof rats, which are much more common further south and rarely the issue here.

House mice are also far more tolerant of close contact with humans than Norway rats. That is part of why a mouse problem can go on for weeks inside an occupied kitchen, while a rat, more cautious by nature, tends to stay confined to less-trafficked areas like a basement or crawl space even in a home with heavy activity.

House mice, by contrast, will go anywhere a quarter-inch gap lets them: wall cavities, kitchen cabinets, attic spaces, and areas well above ground level. A mouse heard scratching in an upstairs ceiling is plausible; a Norway rat up there is much less likely.

  • Norway rats: ground level, basements, foundations, yard burrows.
  • House mice: anywhere, including upper walls, attics and cabinets.
  • Norway rats: larger, blunt-ended droppings, often fewer in number.
  • House mice: small, rice-grain droppings, often scattered more widely.
If the sound is overhead in an upstairs ceiling, that points away from a Norway rat, not toward one.

Why the distinction changes the inspection.

A suspected Norway rat call starts the inspection at the foundation perimeter and yard, looking for burrows, foundation cracks and basement sill plate gaps first. A suspected mouse call starts with the full exterior, since mice are not limited to ground level the way Norway rats are.

Getting this right from the first phone call means the technician arrives already knowing where to look first, rather than starting from zero. Rodent inspection and estimates covers this full process regardless of which animal turns out to be active.

Getting the species wrong at the start of an inspection is not just a labeling error. A technician who treats a rat problem as a mouse problem may check gaps too small to matter for the actual animal present, or miss the foundation-line sealing a rat infestation specifically requires. That is why confirming the species correctly from droppings, sounds, and travel pattern comes before any trap or seal decision is made.

Treatment differences that matter.

Trap size and placement differ for each animal, and so do the sealing materials. A gap sized to stop a mouse, about a quarter inch, is not automatically enough to stop a Norway rat, which needs closer to a half-inch gap sealed. Rodent exclusion and entry point sealing is scoped to the animal actually confirmed, not a generic one-size patch.

Bait station placement differs too. Rat bait stations go along exterior travel routes close to the foundation, since rats are more cautious about entering a structure than mice and spend more time along perimeter paths. Mouse bait stations are more often placed inside, closer to the nesting and food areas mice are already comfortable using.

Quick reference.

The fastest way to tell them apart.

  • Location: ground level and basements point to Norway rat; anywhere, including upstairs, points to mouse.
  • Droppings: raisin-sized and blunt-ended is Norway rat; rice-grain-sized and pointed is mouse.
  • Noise: a heavier thump or scratch at night often signals Norway rat; lighter scurrying often signals mouse.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings or nests. That can put contaminated dust in the air you breathe.
  • Air out a closed attic, garage or crawl space for at least 30 minutes before you go in to work.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Spray the area with disinfectant, wait roughly five minutes, then wipe it up with paper towels instead of dry material.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • If you develop fever, muscle aches or trouble breathing in the weeks after cleaning up rodent droppings, tell a doctor about the exposure. We are not medical professionals and cannot judge individual health risk.

Common questions

Are roof rats ever a concern in Baltimore County?

Occasionally, but they are far less common here than Norway rats, which are the dominant rat species in this region.

Can both mice and Norway rats be active in the same house at once?

Yes, it happens, especially in older homes with multiple types of gaps. The inspection checks for both rather than assuming only one is present.

Does the treatment cost differ between the two?

Scope and materials can differ slightly, but pricing is explained after the inspection confirms what is actually active, not estimated from a phone description alone.
