Every year, calls about scratching in the walls climb sharply once nighttime temperatures in Baltimore County drop consistently into the 40s. That is not a coincidence. House mice are poor at regulating body heat in cold weather, and a sealed-up house with a warm basement or kitchen is simply a better place to spend October through February than a hedge row or woodpile.

The timing is predictable, even if the exact date isn't.

Maryland's climate gives us a real autumn, not the gradual, year-round pressure some southern states see. That means Pikesville's mouse calls follow a seasonal curve: relatively quiet through the summer, then a sharp rise once the first hard frost hits, typically sometime in October or early November depending on the year.

What actually triggers the shift is a combination of consistently cold overnight temperatures and the disappearance of easy outdoor food sources. Both tend to happen together in this area once nighttime lows settle into the 30s on a regular basis, rather than dipping there only occasionally. A single cold night rarely causes the shift on its own; it is the sustained pattern that matters.

The mice themselves do not wait for a calendar date. They respond to the actual drop in overnight temperature, which is why a mild fall can push the seasonal spike later, and an early cold snap can bring it forward by weeks.

What changes about where they look to get in.

In warm months, mice are often content foraging outdoors near a foundation, shed or woodpile. Once temperatures drop, the same gaps they ignored all summer become the entry point of choice: a quarter-inch gap under a garage door, a cracked foundation vent, or an unsealed dryer vent all suddenly matter in a way they did not in July.

Outdoor foraging in warm months also means mice spend less time testing a house's exterior for weaknesses, since food and shelter are both available without needing to get inside. Once outdoor options thin out in the fall, the same gap that sat untested all summer finally gets discovered, not because it changed, but because the animal's priorities did.

  • Garage door sweeps that have worn thin over the summer.
  • Foundation cracks that opened slightly during summer heat and have not been patched.
  • Dryer vents and utility penetrations without a tight-fitting cover.
  • Firewood stacked directly against the house, giving mice a bridge to the siding.
The gap that mattered nothing in July becomes the whole problem in November.

Why sealing before the frost works better than sealing after.

Once mice are established inside a wall cavity or basement for the winter, removing them takes longer and the house has already been breached. Rodent exclusion and entry point sealing done in September or early October, before the seasonal push begins, closes the door before most mice try it rather than after.

Homeowners who call after the first hard frost rather than before it are not necessarily too late. Sealing still stops new animals from getting in even after a frost, and combined with trapping, it resolves an active problem on a similar timeline either way. The main advantage of acting earlier is avoiding the indoor activity altogether, rather than responding to it after the fact.

That is not always possible, and a lot of our fall calls come in after activity has already started. In that case, mouse control still follows the same inspect, trap, seal sequence, just starting from an active problem instead of a preventive one.

What this looks like on a real Pikesville house.

Older homes with masonry or stone foundations that have settled over decades can show activity earliest in the fall, since their gaps may already be slightly larger than a newer home's tighter foundation. Houses with finished basements sometimes mask early signs longer, since the sounds are muffled behind finished walls until the scratching gets loud enough to notice.

Older homes tend to show this pattern slightly earlier in the season than newer construction. Masonry and stone foundations simply develop more seam gaps over time than the tighter, more recently poured foundations common in newer subdivisions.

A practical fall checklist.

Before the first cold snap.

  • Walk the foundation perimeter looking for gaps wider than a pencil.
  • Check the garage door sweep for wear or gaps at the corners.
  • Move firewood and dense landscaping away from direct contact with the siding.
  • Call for an inspection in September if you have had activity in past winters.

Before you call: safe steps for rodent signs

General safety practice drawn from CDC guidance on rodents. It is meant to keep you safe before any professional arrives.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Ventilate a shut-up space first. Half an hour of airing out is the CDC's starting point.
  • Wear rubber or plastic gloves, and wash your hands well afterward, even if you wore gloves.
  • Wet droppings with a disinfectant or a bleach solution and let it soak for about five minutes before wiping with paper towels.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Common questions

Why does activity spike so suddenly instead of building gradually?

Mice respond to the actual drop in overnight temperature rather than the calendar, so a sharp cold snap can trigger a sudden wave of indoor-seeking activity within days.

Does sealing in fall actually prevent the problem, or just delay it?

Sealing before the seasonal push closes the specific gaps mice would otherwise use that winter. It does not make a house permanently rodent-proof forever, but it meaningfully reduces that season's risk.

Is a mild winter less of a rodent risk?

It can shift the timing later and reduce the intensity somewhat, but Baltimore County still gets cold enough most winters for mice to seek shelter indoors regardless.
