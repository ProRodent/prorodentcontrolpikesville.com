What a Norway rat burrow looks like.

A burrow entrance that shows fresh, loose soil scattered just outside it, rather than soil that has settled and grown a thin layer of moss or grass, is a reliable sign of current use rather than an old, abandoned hole. Checking for this kind of fresh disturbance is a quick way to tell an active burrow from one that was dug months ago and never reused.

An active burrow entrance is typically about two to four inches across, with smooth, packed-down edges from regular use, often with a slightly worn path leading to and from it. An inactive or abandoned burrow tends to have looser edges, sometimes partially collapsed, and no worn travel path.

Where burrows tend to show up.

  • Along fence lines, especially where vegetation is thick.
  • Under shed foundations and other outbuildings.
  • Near woodpiles or stacked materials that sit directly on the ground.
  • Close to compost areas or spots where bird feeders have scattered seed.
A worn path leading to a burrow means it is active right now, not an old, abandoned hole.

Why outdoor activity matters even if the house seems fine.

Multiple burrow entrances connected by worn surface paths usually indicate an established colony with more than one adult rat, since a single animal typically maintains just one or two entrances to its burrow system rather than an extensive network. The size and connectivity of the burrow system is a reasonable, if rough, indicator of how large the outdoor population actually is.

Norway rats that have an established, comfortable burrow outdoors do not necessarily move indoors immediately. A yard with active burrows close to the foundation is still a strong predictor of an indoor call within weeks, since the animals are already that close and foundation gaps are common on older homes.

Catching outdoor activity early and addressing it, rather than waiting for scratching inside the house, is one of the more effective ways to avoid a bigger, more involved indoor job later.

What a yard-focused visit involves.

A yard visit also checks compost piles, bird feeder areas, and any spot where pet food or birdseed accumulates on the ground, since these are reliable food sources that can sustain a burrow population independent of anything happening inside the house. Removing or securing these food sources is often as important to long-term control as treating the burrows themselves.

Backyard and outdoor rodent control starts by locating active burrows and the harborage, woodpiles, sheds, dense hedges, that is supporting them. Active burrows are treated and, where it makes sense, collapsed once cleared, and tamper-resistant bait stations are placed only where pets and children cannot reach them.

The same visit checks the foundation line for gaps, since a yard with active Norway rat burrows close to the house is exactly the situation where rodent exclusion and entry point sealing matters most as a preventive step, not an afterthought.

Norway rats will also use a deck, shed, or patio as instant overhead cover for a burrow entrance, since the structure above hides the hole from aerial predators and makes the immediate area feel safer to the animal. A burrow found tucked against a deck support post is one of the more common configurations we see in yards across this area.

Reducing the cover that attracts burrows in the first place.

Moving woodpiles and dense landscaping even a couple of feet away from direct contact with the foundation, keeping compost areas managed, and cleaning up spilled bird seed regularly all reduce the conditions that make a yard attractive for burrowing in the first place.

Gravel or stone border strips along a foundation, a few feet wide, make the immediate perimeter less appealing than dense mulch or thick groundcover. Loose soil and organic material are both easier to burrow into, and they offer cover that bare gravel does not provide in the same way.

A yard walk-around checklist.

What to look for outside.

  • Burrow holes two to four inches across with worn travel paths.
  • Woodpiles or stacked materials sitting directly on bare ground.
  • Dense hedges or ground cover in direct contact with the foundation.
  • Spilled bird seed or unmanaged compost near the house.

Before you call: safe steps for rodent signs

General safety practice drawn from CDC guidance on rodents. It is meant to keep you safe before any professional arrives.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Air out a closed attic, garage or crawl space for at least 30 minutes before you go in to work.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Wet droppings with a disinfectant or a bleach solution and let it soak for about five minutes before wiping with paper towels.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • Flu-like symptoms or shortness of breath after rodent exposure are a reason to see a doctor and mention the cleanup. Health questions belong with a physician, not a pest company.

Common questions

Does a burrow in the yard mean rats are already in the house?

Not necessarily, but it is a strong warning sign, especially if the burrow is close to the foundation, and worth addressing before it progresses indoors.

Can I fill in a burrow myself?

You can fill an inactive, abandoned burrow, but an active one should be treated first, since simply filling it in does not remove the animal and it will often just dig a new entrance nearby.

Are burrows more common near Quarry Lake or Blue Stone Park?

Properties backing onto wooded or water-adjacent green space anywhere in our coverage area do tend to see more outdoor burrow activity, since the cover and moisture support larger populations nearby.
