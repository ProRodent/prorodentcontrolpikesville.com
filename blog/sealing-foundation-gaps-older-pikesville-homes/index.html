Many older houses have stone, block or early poured-concrete foundations, and those age differently than a modern slab. Mortar joints shrink and crack over sixty or seventy years in ways that open gaps exactly the size a mouse or Norway rat needs.

Older homes were often built with a combination of stone and mortar rather than a single poured concrete foundation. That mixed construction tends to develop more individual seams over time than a single continuous pour would.

Why older foundations develop more gaps.

Freeze-thaw cycles, normal settling, and decades of minor shifting all work on a masonry foundation gradually. A gap that started as a hairline crack decades ago can, after enough winters, be wide enough for a mouse to use, and a wider failure at a mortar joint can eventually admit a Norway rat.

Settling is rarely uniform across a single foundation either. One corner of a house can shift slightly more than another depending on soil composition beneath that specific section, which is why gaps often appear first at corners or wherever a porch, addition, or utility penetration interrupts the original foundation line.

This is not a defect specific to any one house. It is simply what happens to masonry over a long enough time, and it is one reason older homes are more likely to have rodent entry points than newer construction.

Where to actually look.

  • Mortar joints at the foundation's visible exterior face, especially near grade level.
  • Foundation vents, which often have damaged or missing screens on older homes.
  • The sill plate where framing meets the top of the foundation wall.
  • Utility penetrations added after original construction, like a later HVAC line or cable run.
A hairline crack from decades ago can be a mouse-sized gap by now, and nobody did anything wrong to get there.

Why caulk is not a real fix here.

Caulk's failure is also gradual rather than sudden, which is part of the problem. It looks intact for a season or two even as it slowly separates from one side of the gap. A homeowner who caulked a crack a couple of years ago and never checked back on it may have no idea the seal has already failed.

Caulk is flexible and easy to apply, which is exactly why it fails against rodents. A mouse or rat can chew through standard caulk in days, and on an older masonry foundation, caulk also does not bond well to aged, slightly crumbling mortar the way it would to clean modern concrete.

The materials that actually hold up are mortar patch for masonry cracks, galvanized hardware cloth for vents and larger gaps, and sheet metal flashing at sill plate joints. None of these are difficult to apply correctly, but they take more care than a tube of caulk, which is part of why DIY sealing attempts on older foundations often need to be redone.

Hardware cloth also needs to be fastened well below grade when sealing a gap at ground level, not just pressed into the visible opening. A determined rodent will dig along the edge of a barrier that only covers the surface rather than extending below the frost line where the actual tunneling would happen.

Timing it with other home maintenance.

If you are already planning foundation, drainage or landscaping work on an older Pikesville property, that is a good time to add a rodent exclusion check. Contractors are already working at the foundation line, and new gaps from that work are easier to catch and seal immediately rather than discovered months later.

What a proper inspection catches that a quick look misses.

A full rodent inspection and estimates visit checks the entire foundation perimeter, not just the side facing the street or the side with an obvious crack. Gaps on the back or side of a house are just as common and just as often missed by a homeowner's own walk-around.

Once every gap is found, rodent exclusion and entry point sealing addresses them with the right material for each: mortar where mortar failed, metal where metal is needed, never a universal caulk bead treated as if it solves every kind of gap.

A homeowner's walk-around checklist.

Before calling for a full inspection.

  • Walk the entire foundation perimeter, not just the visible street-facing side.
  • Look closely at any foundation vent screens for tears or gaps.
  • Note any recent cracking you have not seen before.
  • Check where utility lines enter the foundation for unsealed gaps.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

General safety practice drawn from CDC guidance on rodents. It is meant to keep you safe before any professional arrives.

  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings or nests. That can put contaminated dust in the air you breathe.
  • Open doors or vents and let an enclosed space air out for 30 minutes or more before cleanup.
  • Put on gloves before touching anything contaminated, then wash your hands thoroughly when you finish.
  • Wet droppings with a disinfectant or a bleach solution and let it soak for about five minutes before wiping with paper towels.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • If you develop fever, muscle aches or trouble breathing in the weeks after cleaning up rodent droppings, tell a doctor about the exposure. We are not medical professionals and cannot judge individual health risk.

Common questions

Is an older foundation always going to have gaps eventually?

Some degree of mortar aging is normal over many decades, but regular sealing and periodic inspection keeps it from becoming a rodent entry point.

Can I patch a foundation crack myself?

You can for very small cosmetic cracks, but anything wide enough to be a potential rodent gap needs the right material, mortar or metal, applied correctly, which is worth having inspected first.

Does sealing the foundation affect home value or inspections?

Properly sealed foundation gaps are generally viewed positively in a home inspection, since unsealed gaps are themselves sometimes flagged as a maintenance item.
