Hiring someone to deal with mice or rats in your home is a decision most people make only after the problem has already started to bother them, which can mean less time spent researching than they would like. A few direct questions, asked before booking, clarify what you are actually getting.

Does the visit start with an inspection or a guess?

A plan built from a phone description alone, without ever walking the property, is more likely to miss something than one built from an actual inspection. Ask directly whether pricing and the treatment plan come before or after a technician has physically looked at the property.

A phone-only quote also cannot account for a home's specific construction type. A flat per-visit price quoted before anyone has seen the property is often either padded to cover the unknown, or unrealistically low in a way that gets revised once a technician actually arrives. Either outcome is a worse starting point than an honest, inspection-based estimate from the first visit.

Does the plan include sealing, or just trapping?

Trapping without sealing clears what is inside today but does nothing about the entry point. Ask specifically whether exclusion and sealing are part of the plan, and what materials are used, steel and mortar hold up far longer than caulk alone.

Ask specifically what materials are used for sealing, since the answer reveals a lot about how seriously a company treats the exclusion half of the job. Steel wool and hardware cloth, used correctly, hold up for years; caulk or expanding foam alone, while fine for cosmetic gaps, does not resist a determined rodent and tends to need redoing within a season or two.

  • Does the visit start with a real inspection, not just a phone estimate?
  • Is sealing included, and with what materials?
  • Is pricing explained before work begins, based on what was actually found?
  • Is there a follow-up visit to confirm the work held?
A plan with no sealing step is only solving half the job.

Is pricing explained before work starts?

A technician should be able to tell you what was found and what it will cost before any treatment or sealing work begins, not after. Surprise charges for work discovered mid-visit are a reasonable thing to ask about and rule out ahead of time.

A reputable technician should also be willing to show you the specific gaps or signs found during the inspection, rather than simply stating a diagnosis and moving straight to a price. Being shown the actual sill plate gap, the actual droppings pattern, or the actual chewed insulation builds a level of trust that a verbal summary alone does not, and it is a reasonable thing to ask for if it is not offered.

Is there a follow-up visit built in?

A single visit with no plan to check back is a weaker guarantee than a job that includes a scheduled follow-up to confirm traps are clear and seals are holding. Ask what happens if activity continues after the first visit.

Ask what happens if activity continues after the first visit. A company confident in its own work should have a clear answer, whether that is an included follow-up within a set window or a straightforward return-visit policy, rather than treating a callback as an unusual or unwelcome request.

Does the company know the local housing stock?

Baltimore County's mix of older masonry-foundation homes and newer construction means the common entry points differ by house age and style. A technician who can speak specifically to what tends to show up on an older Pikesville foundation versus newer construction is drawing on real local pattern recognition, not a generic script.

A technician unfamiliar with the difference between a slab foundation and a crawl space, or between masonry and wood-frame construction, is more likely to apply a generic approach that misses the gaps specific to your actual house. Asking a company how long it has worked in the local area is one simple way to gauge this before committing to a visit.

How fast can someone actually come out?

A company that cannot give you a rough same-day or next-day window during an active problem is worth weighing against other options. That is especially true if the activity is heavy or the smell has become a daily issue in the home, rather than a minor, occasional sighting.

Response time matters more for an active problem than a preventive check. Ask directly whether the line is answered around the clock and whether same-day visits are realistic, rather than assuming a quoted response time applies to every situation.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Skip the broom and the vacuum on dry droppings. Stirring them up is how dust gets airborne.
  • Air out a closed attic, garage or crawl space for at least 30 minutes before you go in to work.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Sources and standards

Where the safety and prevention advice here comes from. These are public documents, not endorsements of any company. Reading the originals is the best way to confirm anything we say here.

  • CDC: How to Clean Up After Rodents: Plain safety steps for handling droppings and nesting material, built around wetting first and never dry sweeping.

Common questions

Is it normal for pricing to change after the inspection?

Yes, a realistic quote reflects what the technician actually finds, which is why pricing typically follows the inspection rather than preceding it.

Should I be worried about a company that only offers trapping?

It is worth asking why sealing is not included. Trapping alone addresses the current animals but not the entry point, so understand what you are and are not getting.

Does a follow-up visit cost extra?

That varies by provider; ask specifically whether a follow-up to confirm the work held is included in the original price or billed separately.
